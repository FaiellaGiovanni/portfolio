## Overview

For EML 3234, our team of six researched the material behind one of the most demanding parts of a jet engine: the turbine disk. Turbine disks connect the blades to the shaft and spin at **10,000–15,000 RPM** while facing extreme heat, high rotational stress, and long-term creep and fatigue. We studied why **Inconel 718**, a nickel-based superalloy, has been the industry standard for this job for decades.

## My role

- **Research:** I researched the application side of the project: how turbine disks work in a jet engine, and Inconel 718's track record and credibility in the aerospace industry.
- **Presentation:** I presented the poster to the class at a poster exhibition alongside my teammates.

## Design constraints

- High temperatures, large centrifugal stresses and repeated thermal cycling, all at the same time
- High temperatures sharply accelerate creep, which raises the risk of failure
- The material also has to stay commercially practical in cost and weight, and be manufacturable into intricate shapes

## What we found

- **Composition:** about 51% nickel and 18% chromium, with niobium, molybdenum, titanium and aluminum. Nickel provides corrosion resistance, chromium adds oxidation resistance, and niobium enables the γ″ strengthening phase.
- **Processing:** vacuum induction melting (VIM) keeps the alloy clean, then vacuum arc remelting (VAR) makes it more uniform and removes defects. A precipitation-hardening (aging) heat treatment then gives the alloy its strength.
- **Why it resists creep:** the aging treatment forms fine γ″ precipitates that block dislocation motion. They stay stable at high temperature, so the alloy keeps its strength at roughly 50–60% of its melting temperature.
- **Limitation:** above about 650 °C the γ″ phase coarsens and creep resistance drops, but most commercial jet engines operate below that threshold.

## Conclusion

Inconel 718 is expensive, heavy and hard to manufacture. In turbine disks, where parts face extreme heat, stress and thermal cycling, those trade-offs are worth it. The main lesson: choosing a material isn't about one property. It's about how the material performs across real-world conditions, with reliability and safety first.
